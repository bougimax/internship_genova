\section{Introduction}

This report summarizes my internship at the \emph{CNR IMATI} in Genova, Italy. It was a pleasure to share those three months with Marco, Marco, and the team (where there are three other Marcos)!

My internship focused on computational geometry, the field in which both Marco Attene and Marco Livesu are
working. Recently, Lorenzo Diazzi, Marco A., et al. published an article \cite{diazzi:cdt} discussing constrained Delaunay tetrahedralization. So far, they have developed a robust tetrahedralization pipeline using robust geometric predicates developed by Marco Attene. However, they still lacked a refinement process for the tetrahedralized output that other well-known software like TetGen \cite{si:tetgen} or CGAL \cite{cgal:eb24b} provide.

Constrained Delaunay tetrahedralization (CDT) has a wide range of applications. It is commonly used in
physics-based simulations such as the finite element method, solutions of partial differential equations, or
ray tracing. However, these simulations and applications require high-quality meshes, which raw CDTs often
fail to provide. Therefore, a refinement process is essential to improve the overall mesh quality and ensure
the robustness and accuracy of the simulations.

The objective of this internship was to design and validate a greedy algorithm for refining Constrained Delaunay Tetrahedralizations (CDTs). We drew major inspiration from the article \cite{hu:tetwild}, which already proposed a refinement process but allowed slight deformation of the input surface. In our case, the constraints are strict, meaning we cannot remove an input vertex or change the geometry of the faces. We are only allowed to add vertices, which may lie on the surface of the model only if they do not change the original surface geometry.

The plan of this report is to first present some preliminaries and the state of the art, then to present the refinement process, and finally to compare it with other existing processes.

\subsection*{State of the Art}

The state of the art, for us, is composed of two pieces of work:

\emph{TetGen}~\cite{si:tetgen} is software published in 2015 by Hang Si, which is still a reference regarding constrained Delaunay tetrahedralization. Similar to our project, TetGen has a refinement process at the end of their pipeline where they apply local operations. The issue is that TetGen is not very robust and often crashes or does not converge, thus never finishing. One of the objectives of our algorithm is to be robust.

\emph{Tetrahedral meshing in the wild (TetWild)}~\cite{hu:tetwild} is an article published in 2018 by Hu et al. in which they introduce the Dirichlet energy that we are also using in this project, along with a first set of local operations. In their case, they allowed themselves to slightly deform the geometry of the input, thereby achieving the ability to "correct" some intrinsic malformations of the input (e.g., the model has a very sharp needle). This is something we want to avoid, so one of our other objectives is to be strict on the boundary and not allow any deformation. This may lead to having some elements that cannot be refined.

In the paper, \emph{Tetwild} implement the edge split, the edge collapse, some face swaps and the vertex
smoothing. More precisely, the face swaps they implemented were 3-2, 4-4, 5-6 bistellar flips, which are
particular and precise flips. In our case we will only use 2-3 bistellar flips (§\ref{sec:faceSwap}) and I
will introduce the edge swap (§\ref{sec:edgeSwap}) later on which is a more general swap/flip method. In our
algorithm we are also introducing tetrahedron collapse (§\ref{sec:tetrahedronCollapse}). Finally in our case
all operations can't move any input vertices around.

On the other hand, the \emph{CDT} software published by Lorenzo Diazzi, Marco A., et al.~\cite{diazzi:cdt} is robust and exact on the boundaries but produces low-quality meshes, whereas \emph{TetGen} and \emph{TetWild} produce high-quality meshes. The goal is to offer a refinement process that is still exact and robust to produce high-quality meshes for \emph{CDT}.



\section{Preliminary}

\subsection{Piecewise Linear Complex}

In three dimensions, piecewise linear complexes are extremely flexible representations that allow to model not only 3D surfaces, but also more generic collections of 2D "constraints" that we may want to keep in the final 3D mesh.

\begin{definition}[Piecewise Linear Complex]

	A Piecewise Linear Complex (PLC) $X$ is a finite set of vertices, segments and facets of $\R^3$. A facet
	$f$ is a polygonal region that can have holes and any number of sides, and so is not necessarly convex.
	Then $X$ must respect those two rules:

	\begin{itemize}

		\item $X$ must be closed by intersection, it means that when intersecting two elements of $X$ the
		      intersection is either empty or is an element of $X$.

		\item Every facet of $X$ must be planar.

	\end{itemize}

\end{definition}

\subsection{Delaunay's triangulation}

\begin{definition}[Simplex]

	A simplex $\sigma$ of dimension $d$ is the convex hull of $\left(v_0, v_1, \cdots, v_d\right)$ points of
	$\R^{n}$, such that $(v_1 - v_0, \cdots, v_d - v_0)$ are linearly independant. The $v_{i}$'s are called
	the vertices of $\sigma$, and we say that $\sigma$ is spanned by $V := \left\{v_0, \cdots, v_d \right\}$.
	A face of $\sigma$ is a simplex spanned by a subset of $V$, we denote the fact that $\tau$ is a face of
	$\sigma$ by writing $\tau\leq\sigma$.

\end{definition}

\begin{remark}

	In dimension 1 a simplex is an segment, in dimension 2 it's a triangle and in dimension 3 it's a tetrahedron.

\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Simplicial complex]

	Let $K$ be a set of simplices, we say that $K$ is a simplicial complex if:

	\begin{itemize}

		\item If $\sigma\in K$ then for all $\tau$ such that $\tau\leq\sigma$ we have $\tau\in K$.

		\item Let $\sigma, \sigma'\in K$ then $\sigma\cap\sigma'$ is either empty or a face of both.

	\end{itemize}

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}

	Let $K$ be a simplicial complex, and let $B\subseteq K$:

	\begin{itemize}

		\item The \emph{closure} of $B$ is : $$\Cl{B} := \left\{\tau\in K\; |\; \exists\sigma\in B,
			      \tau\leq\sigma\right\}$$

		\item The \emph{star} of $B$ is : $$\St{B} := \left\{\sigma\in K\; |\; \exists\tau\in B,
			      \tau\leq\sigma\right\}$$

		\item The \emph{link} of $B$ is : $$\Lk{B} := \Cl{\St{B}} \backslash \St{\Cl{B}}$$

		\item We also define for simplicity the \emph{vertices} of $B$ that are : $$\Vert{B} := \left\{v\in B\;|\; \dim v = 0\right\}$$ and the
		      \emph{edges} of $B$ which are : $$\Edges{B} := \left\{e\in B\;|\; \dim e = 1\right\}$$

	\end{itemize}

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Some additional notation may be useful in the following, let $K$ be a simplicial complex:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item We define the incident \underline{V}ertex \underline{T}etrahedra's of $v\in K$ where $\dim v =
			      1$ to be:
		      $$\VT{v} := \left\{t\in K\;|\;\dim t = 3 \text{ and } v\in\Cl{\left\{t\right\}}\right\}$$
		\item We define the incident \underline{E}dge \underline{T}etrahedra's of $e\in K$ where $\dim e =
			      2$ to be:
		      $$\ET{e} := \left\{t\in K\;|\;\dim t = 3 \text{ and } e\in\Cl{\left\{t\right\}}\right\}$$
	\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Delaunay triangulation]

	For $P\subseteq\R^{d}$, a valid Delaunay triangulation is a  simplicial complex $\DT{P}$ such
	that for any $\sigma\in\DT{P}$, the interior of the \emph{circum-hypersphere} of $\sigma$ is empty. The later condition is often denoted as the Delaunay condition.

\end{definition}

\begin{remark}

	In our case we'll only focus on dimension 3, in this case a Delaunay tetrahedrisation exists and is
	guaranteed to be unique if and only if the points are non coplanar and if no 5 points are cospherical.

\end{remark}

\subsection{Constrained Delaunay tetrahedrisation}

While a Delaunay triangulation provides a structure over a set of points, a "constrained" Delaunay triangulation provides a structure over a set of points and a set of "constraints". In 2D, constraints are segments connecting pairs of the input points. In 3D, conjstraints can be either segments and/or polygons that, together with the vertices, form a PLC.

\begin{definition}[Locally Delaunay]
	A triangle $\tau$ is said to be locally Delaunay if one of the two following conditions hold:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item It is on the boundary (\emph{i.e.} it is the face of only one tetrahedron)
		\item It is internal, and the circumsphere of each of its two incident tetrahedra does not contain the
		      opposite vertex of the other
	\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Constrained Delaunay tetrahedrisation]
	Let $X = (V, E, F)$ be a PLC where $V$ is the set of vertices, $E$ is the set of segments, and $F$ is the
	set of polygonal facets. A constrained Delaunay tetrahedrization $C$ of $X$ is a 3D simplicial complex
	subdividing the convex hull of $X$ such that:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The vertex set $V'$ of $C$ contains $V$
		\item Any triangular face of $C$ either is part of a facet in $F$ or is locally Delaunay
	\end{itemize}

\end{definition}

\subsection{Quality Measure}

To measure the quality of our refinement we need a quality measure. A good quality measure should be minimized
on \emph{well-shaped} tetrahedras and becomes really big on \emph{bad-shaped} tetrahedras. Typically a
\emph{well-shaped} tetrahedron would be one close to be a regular tetrahedron, whereas the \emph{bad-shaped}
ones are typically slivers which are very flat tetrahedras that appears in constrained and unconstrained
Delaunay tetrahedrisation (see on figure \ref{fig:sliver}) or the ones with very acute angle.

\input{tikz/example\_sliver}

In our implementation we choosed to use the Dirichlet energy because it's assigning 3 to a regular tetrahedron,
is not upper bounded and assigns very high value to slivers.

\begin{definition}[Edge matrix]

	Let $t$ be a tetrahedron composed of vertices $(\mathbf{x}_i)_{0\leq i\leq 3}\in\R^{3}$, the edge matrix of
	$t$ is defined to be:

	$$T := \biggl( \mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_0\quad \mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_0\quad \mathbf{x}_3 -
		\mathbf{x}_0\biggr)$$

\end{definition}

\begin{remark}

	This definition of the edge matrix is invariant by translation.

\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Tetrahedron deformation]

	Let $t$ be a tetrahedron and $r$ another one that you want to deform $t$ to, let $T$ and respectively $R$
	be the associated edge matrix. We define the deformation matrix to be:

	$$D := TR^{-1}$$

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Dirichlet energy]

	Let $t$ be a tetrahedron and $J$ be the deformation matrix when we deform $t$ to the regular tetrahedron of
	side 1. We define the Dirichlet energy to be:

	$$\mathcal{E}(t) := \frac{\Tr{JJ^{t}}}{\det(J)^{\frac{2}{3}}}$$

\end{definition}

\begin{remark}

	The definition of this energy makes it so that it's invariant by rotation of $t$ and of the regulart
	tetrahedron chosen, this is why we say \emph{the} regular tetrahedron of side 1.

\end{remark}

\begin{remark}

	One may wonder if the order of the vertices in the input tetrahedron $t$ is important, the answer is that
	as soon as the tetrahedron is well-oriented the order does not change the result. If the
	tetrahedron is badly-oriented the determinant at the denominator will be negative and so we just return the
	maximum value because it's out of definition for $\frac{2}{3}$ exponent.

\end{remark}


\section{The algorithm}

\subsection{General Principle}

The algorithm consists of several local operations that I will detail in the following sections. The main function is the global optimization pass, which means it performs all the different local operations in sequence. One can choose to run this optimization pass a fixed number of times or to let it run until convergence (until no operations can be applied to the mesh).

The process for each individual operation pass is quite similar for all of them, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localOperationPass}.

First, there is the simulation pass, which is crucial to determine which element on the mesh we will apply the operation to. Simulating the effect means computing the maximum quality measure locally before and after the hypothetical operation on a specific element. There are several steps to validate whether the element is suitable for the operation. Here they are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The maximum local quality measure must decrease.
	\item The geometry should not be broken (e.g., some tetrahedra may be inverted and thus have negative volume, or the boundary of the mesh may be deformed).
	\item The topology should be preserved (e.g., the manifold structure should be preserved).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{}The simulation must take into account all these conditions, and if an element meets every one of them, it is
then added to a queue of elements to process. The queue is either a priority queue or a random queue (insert
and random pop), for the priority queue we tried two parameter for the priority : the maximum local quality
measure before the operation, or the gap between the maximum local quality measure before and after the
operation. In the we figured by experiments that the second one is better in most of the cases.\label{par:greedyParameters}

\input{tikz/local_operation_pass}

After computing the queue, the process is greedy. We pop an element, and since it is in the queue, we know it is a good element to operate on. We apply the local operation and retrieve both the removed and impacted elements by the operation. These elements are then respectively removed from the queue and re-simulated to determine whether to add them back into the queue or remove them. We always want to maintain the invariant that if an element is in the queue, it is good to operate on.

The difficult parts are often knowing if the operation will break the topology and knowing which elements are impacted by a local operation.

In the following sections, I will present every operation, detailing the impacted elements, topological conditions to check, and what the operation does.

\subsection{Local operations}

\subsubsection{Edge split}

\input{tikz/example\_split}

The edge split as the name suggests is the fact to add a new vertex to an edge and then arrange the geometry
accordingly. For this operation we do not have to worry about the fact that it could break the topology
because it cannot.

By defining $\mathcal{T} := \ET{(u,v)}$ the incident tetrahedra of the edge $(u,v)$. We have that when we
split $(u,v)$ by adding $w$ in the middle of it, each $T\in\mathcal{T}$ will be transformed in the following manner:

$$T := \left\{u,v,x,y\right\} \longrightarrow T' := \left\{u,w,x,y\right\} \text{ and }
	T'' :=\left\{w,v,x,y\right\}$$

Let's define $\mathcal{T}' := \left\{T'\; |\; T\in\mathcal{T}\right\} \cup \left\{T''\; |\; T\in\mathcal{T}\right\}$ the set of transformed tetrahedra. Therefore the operation is valid on the edge $(u,v)$ if:

$$\underset{T'\in\mathcal{T}'}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T')\leq\underset{T\in\mathcal{T}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

Then after applying the operation the edges to update are:

$$E := \Edges{\ET{(u,w)}\cup\ET{(w,v)}}$$

Which are the edges of all the tetrahedra incident to either $(u,w)$ or $(w,v)$.

% $$\left\{e\text{ an edge}\ |\ \exists t\text{ a tetrahedron},\, e<t \text{ and }
% 	\left((u,w)<t\text{ or }(v,w)<t\right)\right\}$$

\subsubsection{Edge collapse}

\input{tikz/example\_collapse}

Rather the intuitive approach to the edge collapse depicted on figure \ref{fig:collapseEdge}, this is one of the hardest operation to approach. In fact the difficulty of this operation is to prevent topological break. Firstly the vertex or the edge that we are
going to remove should not be on the boundary of the model. Then the principal condition to ensure to not
break the topology is the link condition for collapsing $(u,v)$:

\begin{equation}
	\tag{Link condition}
	\Lk{\left\{(u,v)\right\}} = \Lk{\left\{u\right\}} \cap \Lk{\left\{v\right\}}
	\label{eqn:linkCondition}
\end{equation}

Then when collapsing the edge $(u,v)$ on $u$ the incident tetrahedra at the edge $(u,v)$ $\ET{(u,v)}$ will be
removed whereas each $T\in\VT{v}\backslash\ET{(u,v)}$ will be transformed in the following manner:

$$T:=\left\{v,a,b,c\right\}\longrightarrow T':=\left\{u,a,b,c\right\}$$

Therefore the operation is valid if it respects the \ref{eqn:linkCondition} and if:

$$\underset{T\in\VT{v}\backslash\ET{(u,v)}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T')\leq\underset{T\in\VT{v}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

In a second time when collapsing the edge $(u,v)$ on $u$ the removed edges that has to be supressed from the
queue are:

$$R := \Edges{\St{\left\{v\right\}}}$$

and the edges to update are:

$$E := \Edges{\Cl{\Vert{\Cl{\left\{v\right\}}}}} \backslash R$$



\subsubsection{Face swap}\label{sec:faceSwap}

\input{tikz/example\_swap\_face}

The face swap modifies the configuration of two tetrahedra that share a common face $\sigma = (u,v,w)$.

The face swap is defined on two tetrahedra that share a face $\sigma$. Denote those tetrahedra as:

$$T_1 = \left\{u, v, w, a\right\} \text{ and } T_2 = \left\{u, v, w, b\right\}$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the vertices opposite to the shared face $\sigma$ in each tetrahedron.

The face swap replaces $T_1$ and $T_2$ by three new tetrahedra:

$$T'_1 = \left\{a, b, u, v\right\}, \quad T'_2 = \left\{a, b, v, w\right\}, \quad T'_3 = \left\{a, b, w,
	u\right\}$$

These tetrahedra share the edge $(a,b)$ and together fill the same volume as $T_1 \cup T_2$, ensuring the
local topology remains consistent. The operation is then feasible if:

$$\underset{T\in\left\{T'_1,T'_2,T'_3\right\}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\underset{T\in\left\{T_1,T_2\right\}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

An example of such a face swap operation is illustrated in figure \ref{fig:swapFace}.

The faces to update are those of the three new tetrahedras added, which is:

$$F := \left\{\sigma\in\Cl{\left\{T'_1,T'_2,T'_3\right\}}\;|\; \dim \sigma = 2\right\}$$


\subsubsection{Edge swap}\label{sec:edgeSwap}

\input{tikz/example\_swap\_edge}

The edge swap changes the connectivity around an edge $(u,v)$ shared by multiple tetrahedra. It consists of two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Split the edge $(u,v)$ by inserting a new vertex $m$ at its midpoint
	\item Collapse $m$ onto a neighbor vertex $w$ distinct from $u$ and $v$
\end{enumerate}

Let $\mathcal{T} := \ET{(u,v)}$, when collapsing on vertex $w$, each tetrahedron $T := \left\{u,v,a,b\right\}$ that does not contain $w$ is subdivided into two tetrahedra:

$$T \rightarrow T' := \{u, w ,a,b\} \quad \text{and} \quad T'' :=\{v, w ,a,b\}$$

Whereas the tetrahedron that contains the vertex $w$ is removed. Let's define the updated tetrahedra when
collapsing $m$ on $w$:

$$\mathcal{T}_{w} :=
	\left\{T'\;|\;T\in\mathcal{T},\; w\notin T\right\} \cup\left\{T''\;|\;T\in\mathcal{T},\; w\notin
	T\right\}$$

Then concerning the topological point of view we still have to check the link condition when collapsing on $m$
on $w$, we can then define the collapsible vertices to be:

$$\mathcal{C} := \left\{w\in\Vert{\Lk{(u,v)}}\;|\; \Lk{\left\{(m,w)\right\}} = \Lk{\left\{m\right\}} \cap
	\Lk{\left\{w\right\}}\right\}$$


The operation is then valid if:

$$\underset{w\in\mathcal{C}}{\min}\underset{T\in\mathcal{T}_{w}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\underset{T\in\ET{(u,v)}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

Finally there is no edge that is removed except the one we operated on and the edges to update are:

$$E:= \underset{x\in\Vert{\mathcal{T}}}{\bigcup}\left\{(x,y)\;|\; y\text{ is a neighbour of }x\right\}$$

There is an example in figure~\ref{fig:swapEdge}.

\subsubsection{Vertex smoothing}

\input{tikz/example\_smooth\_vertex}

Vertex smoothing is an operation that does not change the connectivity of the mesh. Instead it changes the
geometry, by moving vertices to the barycenter of their neighbour. Let $v$ be a vertex and
$V:=\Vert{\Cl{v}}\backslash\left\{v\right\}$ the neighbour of $v$. Then $v$ is moved to:

$$v' := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in V} u$$

And so each $T := \left\{v,x,y,z\right\}\in\VT{v}$ is transformed to $T' := \left\{v',x,y,z\right\}$ and the
operation is valid if it does not invert any tetrahedron and if:

$$\underset{T\in\VT{v}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T')\leq\underset{T\in\VT{v}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

No vertices have been removed and thus the vertices to update are the ones in $V$.

Here is an example of smoothing a vertex in figure~\ref{fig:smoothVertex} (it is 2D here for simplicity).




\subsubsection{Tetrahedron collapse}\label{sec:tetrahedronCollapse}

\input{tikz/tetrahedra\_collapse}

Slivers as depicted in figure \ref{fig:sliver} can be hard to get rid of. In fact in certain cases, due to the
fact that we are only doing operations if they decrease the maximum quality measures locally, it is hard to
remove slivers in one single operation. This is why creating a new combination of already existing operations
can help to prevent slivers to appear in the final mesh.

A typical example of slivers that were hard to remove were the one near the boundary. Every single operation
would cause to degrade the mesh locally. But seeing further than one operation is possible, and this is what
the tetrahedron collapse does.

The tetrahedron collapse on $T := \left\{u,v,w,x\right\}$ is a series of three operation, which are:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item An edge split on $(u,v)$ by adding $m$
	\item An edge split on $(w,x)$ by adding $m'$
	\item An edge collapse of $m'$ on $m$
\end{enumerate}

By defining $\mathcal{R} := \ET{(u,v)} \backslash \left\{T\right\}$ and $\mathcal{S} := \ET{(w,x)}\backslash
	\left\{T\right\}$ and then define the updated tetrahedra by splitting and collapsing we can define
$\mathcal{R}'$ and $\mathcal{S}'$:

$$\text{For }R := \left\{u,v,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{R}, R\longrightarrow R':=\left\{u,m,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{R}'\text{
		and }R'':=\left\{v,m,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{R}' $$

$$\text{For }S := \left\{w,x,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{S}, S\longrightarrow
	S':=\left\{w,m,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{S}'\text{
		and }S'':=\left\{x,m,a,b\right\}\in\mathcal{S}' $$

Then the operation is valid if:

$$\underset{T\in\mathcal{R}'\cup\mathcal{S}'}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\underset{T\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\left\{T\right\}}{\max}\;\mathcal{E}(T)$$

The link condition also needs to be checked when collapsing the edge $(m,m')$.

Finally the set of tetrahedron to update is $\mathcal{R}'\cup\mathcal{S}'$.

\subsection{Code organizations and specifications}

The GitHub is a fork of the CDT repository of Marco Attene, this way I can have access to the algorithm that
produces constrained Delaunay tetrahedrization. The code in itself is in the \texttt{code/src} directory, the
code that I have written are in the following files:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{optimize.cpp/h}
	\item \texttt{quality\_measure.cpp/h}
	\item \texttt{random\_map.h}
	\item \texttt{updatable\_queue\_template.h}
	\item \texttt{updatable\_priority\_queue.h}
\end{itemize}

Concerning the greedy method to use, we have tried several one. The first thing we tried was to use a vector
instead of a queue, to sort it according to the priority parameter I have decribed in the end of
§~\ref{par:greedyParameters}, and then not updated it and check when popping an element if the element is
still worth to operate on. This method was converging very slowly, so we decided to switch to an updatable
priority queue. The priority queue needs to have an id for each element, then it can attach some data to this
id (e.g. position of the split vertex for an edge ...). The insertion and the removal of an element is done i
n $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ where $n$ is the number of element in the queue and update is done in constant time.
The random queue is composed of a vector containing the element and of an unordered map which maps the
position of each element in a vertex. In this way insertion, removal and update are all done in constant
amortized time.

\section{Conclusion}

To conclude, I think that a good part of the objective is complete. We managed to maintain the robustness of the process in the refinement part while improving the quality of the mesh. We also kept the boundaries of the input untouched as it was stated initially. To accomplish this, I needed to implement simulators for the operations to predict their behavior on the geometry and the topology of the mesh. After that, I needed to actually implement the local operation itself. Still, some things may have to be done, like optimizing the speed or continuing to improve the quality of the mesh. I detail in the following paragraph some ideas we did not have the time to develop that would be interesting to look at in the future.

\subsection*{Possible improvements}

\begin{itemize}

	\item \textbf{Refining some operations}: Some operation designs have not been developed to their full potential. I particularly think of the edge split and the vertex smoothing. For the moment, the split vertex is always placed at the middle of the edge, and the vertex smoothing also always moves the vertex to the barycenter of its neighbors. Another finer option would be to run an algorithm like Newton's method to find the best position for the concerned vertex.

	\item \textbf{Be less strict on the updates}: Currently, the major bottleneck concerning the speed is the
	      number of updates we have to make. In fact, for some operations, the number of calls to the simulation
	      function for the update after operating on one single element can reach thousands of calls. The thing
	      is that, in general, we need to do all those updates because all elements might really be impacted.
	      In reality, it seems that only a very few of those updates are really necessary. These are often the
	      elements of the tetrahedra that are really impacted and not, for example, edges that are connected
	      to one vertex of an impacted tetrahedron (which is the case for every operation using the collapse
	      method). The idea may be to stop updating the queue and, when popping, check that the operation is
	      still good to perform. In this way, we may have more global optimization passes, but they would be
	      much quicker. Concerning the efficiency of this method, we do not think it would affect much
	      because, in the end, it is still a greedy process.

\end{itemize}
